# Daiki Amori

## Basic Information

- **Name:** Daiki Amori
- **Status:** Former Tenebri - left the organisation
- **Former Sin:** Gluttony
- **Current Location:** Tidal Plate - isolated, largely absent from daily life
- **Path:** Akarui
- **Class:** Veil
- **Ability:** All Seeing Eye
- **Family:** Alice Amori (wife), Kaito Amori (son)

---

## Overview

Daiki Amori was the former Sin of Gluttony within the Tenebri—a successor faction member who left the organisation. This is not something people do. Leaving the Tenebri is not a clean process and the reasons for surviving it are not always comfortable ones.

His family had already been sent to the Tidal Plate before he left—the specific consequence-as-management that the organisation uses for members whose commitment it suspects. His son was born on the Tidal Plate. Daiki left the Tenebri afterward, which means he chose his family over the organisation at a point where his family was already beyond his immediate reach, and the cost of leaving was substantial.

He is on the Tidal Plate now. He has been for years. He is not particularly present in Kaito's life, which is not because he does not care—it is because of what his ability does and the specific, exhausting architecture of living with it while trying not to hurt people.

## The World That Made Him

Daiki was not born invisible. He was born noticed—and then systematically made irrelevant.

His family was modest, unremarkable in the grand sweep of things. His father worked two jobs. His mother kept the books. They didn't have much, but they had what mattered: a house with a porch, a dog that greeted him every afternoon, the kind of ordinary happiness that doesn't get written down anywhere.

Except it wasn't ordinary for long.

The first time he understood he was invisible was at ten years old, at a parent-teacher conference. His mother had worked double shifts to afford the shirt he wore that day. She had clean hands and tired eyes and asked polite questions about his arithmetic. The teacher nodded politely, then turned to Daiki's father and said, "Tell me about your business ventures."

Daiki's father was an accountant. He did not have ventures. He had a spreadsheet. But he nodded and played along, because the teacher was looking at him, not his wife, and the conversation was happening in the space his father occupied, not the space his mother occupied.

Daiki sat in the plastic chair, small and unnoticed, and learned the first lesson: attention is power, and power goes to the person the world chooses to see.

The second time was at fifteen, during the regional science fair. Daiki had built a working hydroelectric prototype from scavenged parts, a micro-turbine that spun quietly and generated enough power to light three bulbs. He had spent months on it, measuring water flow, calibrating angles, debugging circuit boards. He had presented it to his class, his voice shaking but clear, explaining Bernoulli's principle in terms they could understand.

The judges walked up, nodded at his display, then asked, "Where's your partner?"

Daiki blinked. "I built it alone."

The judges exchanged glances. One of them said, "We have a team project requirement." They scanned the room, found a pair of boys who were actually there with a team project (a volcano that leaked baking soda vinegar mixture onto the floor), and walked over.

Daiki stood by his prototype, hands in his pockets, holding his breath, waiting for someone to notice that he was actually the one who had built something that worked.

No one did. The trophies went to the volcano boys. Daiki got a participation ribbon.

He didn't mention it at home. He put the ribbon in a drawer. That night, he lay in bed and practiced looking people in the eyes, just looking, memorizing the feeling of someone's attention holding still for a moment before moving on.

He learned the second lesson: if you are not seen, you learn to see.

The third time was at twenty-two, when he stood in the conference room of the tech firm he had joined after graduation. He had spent six months building a network architecture that would reduce latency by 40%. He had presented it to the CTO, who nodded and said, "Show me the numbers."

Daiki did. He had spreadsheets, graphs, projections. The CTO looked at them, then at the senior engineer standing beside him. "Does this seem plausible?"

The senior engineer—older, white, someone the world was already built to see—looked at the slides for three seconds, then said, "I'm not convinced."

The CTO said, "Fair. Let's stick with the current architecture."

Daiki didn't argue. He didn't say anything. He walked back to his desk and opened his laptop and kept building his architecture in private, on his own time, because he knew what would happen if he raised his voice: they would hear him, but they would not believe him. They would see his mouth moving, but they would not hear the words.

That night, he lay awake and practiced seeing. Not just looking—seeing. Reading the room, reading the people, reading the unspoken currents of power and attention. He learned the third lesson: if the world will not see you, you learn to see it first.

The fourth time was at twenty-four, when his father had a heart attack. Daiki was at work when the call came. His mother was sobbing on the other end of the line, saying he needed to come, now, the ambulance took him too fast, she didn't know what to do.

Daiki left work. He drove to the hospital. He found his mother in the waiting room, red-eyed and clutching a plastic cup of bad coffee. She told him the doctor had said his father was stable but in critical condition, that the surgeons were still working, that he should wait here.

Daiki waited. He waited for two hours. He waited while people moved around him, through the lobby, to their destinations. He waited while nurses checked his mother's wrist for her pulse, not his. He waited while a social worker walked up, asked his mother about insurance, and walked away when he saw her confusion.

He stood up. He walked to the nurse's station. He asked for his father's surgeon. The nurse said, "Let me check." She came back five minutes later and said, "The surgeon is in surgery. I'll leave a message."

Daiki didn't ask again. He walked back to his mother and said, "We're going home. We can't wait here for people who don't have time for us."

His mother looked at him like she was seeing him for the first time. "But what if something happens?"

"Then we'll be prepared for it next time," he said. He meant it. He wasn't angry. He was just finished waiting for permission.

He took his mother home. He called the hospital the next morning. He asked for the surgeon's name. He looked up the surgeon's credentials, the hospital's reputation, the typical outcomes for his father's condition. He made spreadsheets. He called other hospitals. He made a plan.

His father survived. The surgeon said it was a miracle. Daiki didn't tell him it wasn't. He just made sure his father had the best care, the best recovery, the best everything—because he learned the fourth lesson: if the world will not protect you, you learn to protect it yourself.

These four moments—ten, fifteen, twenty-two, twenty-four—made Daiki Amori.

They made him believe that visibility is power. That attention is control. That being seen, truly seen, is the only thing that matters.

And when he found out about the Tenebri—about people who could manipulate perception, who could make the world see what they wanted it to see—he didn't think about power as a tool. He thought about it as a right.

All Seeing Eye was not a gift. It was a correction.

## All Seeing Eye

**Path:** Akarui Veil.

**Condition:** The target looks directly into his eyes.

The duration of the gaze determines the degree of control. A brief glance—the accidental eye contact of passing someone in a corridor—produces a mild suggestion-level effect that fades within minutes. Sustained eye contact of several seconds produces significant control over the target's immediate decision-making. Prolonged, unbroken eye contact produces full control for the duration of the contact.

The control is not possession. The target is aware that something is wrong—that their actions are not entirely their own—but cannot override it while the eye contact persists. The awareness is the horror. Being controlled by All Seeing Eye feels like watching yourself do something you would not choose.

**The limitation:** It requires genuine eye contact. A target who closes their eyes, looks away, uses reflective surfaces, or wears appropriate eyewear is protected. The ability requires the target to give him their gaze.

**Living with it:** Daiki wears tinted eyewear in any situation with multiple people present. He avoids sustained eye contact even in one-on-one conversations. He has developed the specific social texture of someone who seems slightly distant—someone who looks at the space beside a person's face rather than their eyes, who positions himself slightly off-axis in conversations. People who know him understand why. People who do not find him unsettling in a way they cannot articulate.

His relationship with Alice is built around this. She knows what his eyes do. She looks at him sometimes when she chooses to—briefly, in controlled circumstances, in moments where the control is welcome rather than terrifying. Their relationship has its own specific architecture that is unlike anything else and is, in its particular way, honest.

## Pride — What It Is

Daiki's Pride is not arrogance. It is not vanity. It is not even ambition in the traditional sense.

Pride for Daiki is the absolute certainty that he, Daiki Amori, deserves to be seen.

Not to be noticed—the way a background character is noticed, remembered only as part of the setting. Not to be acknowledged—the way a functionary is acknowledged, remembered only for the role they occupy.

To be seen. Truly, fully, completely seen. Not just as a role, not just as a function, not just as a person who exists in relation to something more important, but as the central character in his own story.

The Tenebri did not corrupt him into Pride. They recognised it in him and gave it a name, a framework, a community. But Pride was already there, baked into his bones by years of being told his contributions didn't count, his ideas weren't significant, his presence wasn't necessary.

The Tenebri said, "We can give you the power to be seen." And he said, "Yes."

All Seeing Eye is this expressed as ability. The desire to see into people taken to its logical conclusion: looking at them and having them. Not controlling them the way a puppeteer controls strings, but having them recognize you as the center of their world, the only point of reference that matters.

Leaving the Tenebri did not resolve this. It only removed the organisation as the thing containing him.

The danger of Daiki is that he can look like someone who escaped the villains while still carrying the exact hunger that made him belong there. He left the Tenebri not because he rejected their philosophy, but because he outgrew it. The Tenebri offered him a seat at their table. He left because he wanted to build his own table, on his own terms, with his own rules.

This is Pride. Not arrogance, but the refusal to accept a seat at someone else's table. Not vanity, but the certainty that his worth does not depend on anyone else's recognition. Not ambition, but the belief that the world should bend to accommodate his presence, not the other way around.

## His Absence from Kaito's Life

Daiki is present on the Tidal Plate. He is not present in Kaito's daily existence.

This is partly the ability: the logistics of being a father when sustained eye contact with your child risks controlling them are complicated in ways that do not have clean solutions. It is partly the Tenebri: he left, but they did not stop existing, and a former member is not someone the organisation simply forgets. Visibility invites consequences.

It is also, in a way Daiki has not examined cleanly, that he does not know how to be the father of a child he was not present for, who was raised by someone who did it well without him, who is becoming a person Daiki can observe from a distance but has no foundation with.

He watches Kaito. From a distance that feels safe and is actually, in its own way, the All Seeing Eye at a metaphorical level: understanding his son comprehensively from a position of no direct contact. The irony of this is not lost on him. He does nothing with it.

Alice knows where he is. She does not discuss him with Kaito. She will when it becomes necessary. It is not yet necessary.

## What Kaito Does Not Know

Kaito knows he has a father somewhere. He does not know what his father was.

He does not know that the specific sensitivity he has—the ability to read rooms, to feel what people are feeling before they say it, the way emotional states register in him as something almost physical—is not just his own Kyumin sensitivity. It is partially inherited. Daiki's Veil sensitivity runs through the family.

He does not know that the reason his eyes are different from each other—the blue right eye that he has spent fifteen years wanting to correct—is connected to his father's ability in ways that will matter.
